## Ideas so far

Technique: Verbs Before Nouns. These are sparks for Luka to react to; none is chosen.

**[Verb #1]**: Outsmart
_Core Loop_: Each automaton runs on one wrong rule, such as "the last digit decides which number is bigger". He picks numbers that make the rule fail, and the automaton jams. The ultimate trick is getting one to divide by zero, so it spins forever.
_Novelty_: Each enemy is a misconception, and beating it means understanding why it is wrong.

**[Verb #2]**: Track
_Core Loop_: He hunts Kristijan through the town like a detective. Witnesses give number clues: "the even side, past 230 but before 240", "about 150 steps from the well". He narrows down the place on a street that is a number line.
_Novelty_: Ranges, rounding, even and odd become ways of searching. Even and odd are real sides of a street.

**[Verb #3]**: Catch the lie
_Core Loop_: The First Automaton forges the city's ledgers, notices and charts. He finds the line that can't be true, ideally without calculating, and points at it.
_Novelty_: It extends Kristijan's planted mistake from room 7 into a way of playing.

**[Verb #4]**: Teach
_Core Loop_: A small broken automaton follows him and does exactly what it is told, wrongly. He shows it why, with rows of trees rather than a rule, and its behaviour changes.
_Novelty_: He does for it what nobody did for the First Automaton.

**[Verb #5]**: Answer
_Core Loop_: The First Automaton took Kristijan to ask why the machines work. The final meeting is not a fight: it asks, and he is the first person who can answer. This part of the game is collecting those answers.
_Novelty_: It pays off the story's main question, and the villain's ending comes from understanding.

**[Provocation #6]**: No sums
_Core Loop_: The numbers stay tiny, or the game does the arithmetic, so the only hard thing is the idea. He decides what to calculate and why.
_Novelty_: It removes the drill. Open question: how this keeps pillar 3, "every idea lands on paper".

## Research checked (after the sparks, at Luka's prompting)

The sparks above came from design reasoning, not research. This check read abstracts and search summaries, not full papers. Most studies are not of 10-year-olds or of games.

**General findings**

- **Judging without calculating:** relational thinking (Carpenter, Franke & Levi 2003). Pupils use properties of numbers instead of calculating, as in deciding whether `346 + 289 = 345 + 288` is true. The closest match to Luka's point.
- **Concrete first, then symbols:** concreteness fading (Fyfe et al. 2014). Manipulatives have moderate to large effects on retention, but small effects on problem solving, transfer and justification (Carbonneau et al. 2013).
- **Teach first at his age:** across 53 studies, problem solving before instruction wins overall, but for 2nd to 5th graders effects favour instruction first (Sinha & Kapur 2021). This fits his first playtest and pushes against the idea that he works it out before being told (the brief's pillar 2, the GDD's pillar 1).
- **The maths must be the play:** Zombie Division, ages 7–11, already in the brief's research digest.

**The sparks against the evidence**

| Spark | Evidence found | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Catch the lie | Incorrect examples helped grades 4–5 with decimals (Durkin 2009), but gave no added benefit in a later study (Durkin 2012) | Mixed |
| Outsmart | Counterexamples help primary pupils refine conjectures (Komatsu), but Years 4–6 struggled to find them (Widjaja & Vale) | Some, needs support; nothing as a game |
| Teach | Pupils try harder for a teachable agent (Chase et al. 2009, science). No maths study or meta-analysis found. A Swedish grades 5–6 follow-up found no link to test scores (snippet only) | Weak for maths |
| Track | Nothing on detective play. Number-line board games have strong evidence, but in preschoolers (Siegler & Ramani) | None found |
| Answer | Self-explanation search returned nothing | Not checked |
| No sums | Relational thinking supports judging without calculating. Nothing tests the game doing the sums | Indirect |

**By topic**

- **Order of operations:** a left-to-right bias is documented, in college students. Mnemonics are criticised. No strong trial of a better method found.
- **Distributivity:** area models help pupils see why, but the evidence is small-scale.
- **Mean:** children aged 9–14 hold five different ideas of "average" (Mokros & Russell 1995); multiple models are recommended.
- **Remainders:** in a 1993 study only 45% of grades 6–8 pupils solved one remainder problem type sensibly. Learning the algorithm helped division but not remainder problems. Sharing problems were easier than grouping ones.
- **Graphs:** reading the data is easiest, then between the data, then beyond it (Curcio; Friel et al. 2001).
- **Place value:** nothing useful in this check.

## Concept: the trail and the little automaton

**In one line:** he tracks Kristijan through the walkable town with a small automaton at his heels, reading clues that only someone who understands can read.

The research supports the teaching ladder below, not the detective format. Track is a design bet. Names and story beats are placeholders, and Croatian lines are Luka's.

### Opening

**[Story #1]**: The knock is the code
_Core Loop_: The current ending has someone knocking behind the foundry gate, five times, then eight (`hr.ts:427`). That is `5 · 8 = 40`, the gate's own sign. He opens it straight away.
_Novelty_: It resolves the gate without a new tool. The line "needs a tool you don't have" (`hr.ts:635`) has to change.

**[Story #2]**: The foundry is empty
_Core Loop_: Kristijan has been moved, and a notice says so with polite menace. The knocker is a tiny automaton Kristijan built from scrap. It knows one step: knock.
_Novelty_: The villain's "corrected" joke now applies to the hostage.

**[Companion #3]**: It imprints on him
_Core Loop_: It follows the first person who opened the door, like a duckling, and he names it.
_Novelty_: Naming is already his reward for proving himself in the first part.

### What the little automaton does

**[Companion #4]**: It carries Kristijan's lessons
_Core Loop_: Kristijan hid the whys in the city, away from the First Automaton. Each one he finds is a cylinder the little one plays, showing the idea with objects before he has to use it.
_Novelty_: Teaching first, as the research says for his age, delivered as a story reward.

**[Companion #5]**: It tries, and gets it wrong
_Core Loop_: After a lesson it applies the idea literally and makes the classic mistake: it walks to the wrong house, reading left to right. He spots where it went wrong and fixes it.
_Novelty_: Mistakes are funny and belong to it. The `?` hint stays truthful, and what it says as a guess must look like a guess, so it can't confuse a child who needs help.

**[Companion #6]**: It knocks for him
_Core Loop_: It knocks on the door he picks, and a wrong door brings a grumpy neighbour, a funny line and sometimes a hint.
_Novelty_: A wrong idea has a visible consequence in the world.

**[Companion #7]**: The spring
_Core Loop_: Each knock uses some of its spring, and so does asking the abacus to do a long calculation. Thinking is free. When it runs down he carries it to Faust.
_Novelty_: It stops guessing door to door, the way he mashed dice, and makes reasoning pay without forbidding calculation. Nothing is lost, and carrying it is cute.

**[Companion #8]**: Parts go on it
_Core Loop_: Parts buy a longer spring, a lamp, a hat, new legs.
_Novelty_: A new sink for the economy, and a reason to fight.

### Teaching ladder for every new idea

1. A cylinder shows it with objects (teach first, concrete).
2. He does it with objects and tiny numbers.
3. The same with numbers, written in the notebook (fading, and pillar 3).
4. The little one gets it wrong; he finds where and fixes it (wrong examples beside right ones).
5. A clue in the town needs it, with numbers too big to bother calculating, so reasoning wins (relational thinking).

### Clues by unit

Kristijan signals so that only someone who understands can read it, which is already in the narrative. The First Automaton's helpers follow the steps and go wrong.

| Unit | In the town |
|---|---|
| 1. N, N₀, even and odd | Street sides are even and odd. Houses start at 1; a lift's ground floor is 0. |
| 2. Big numbers, powers of ten | An archive where a number is an address: 34 072 is hall 3, row 4, shelf 0, box 7, folder 2. Swap two digits and you're elsewhere. A hall holds 10⁴ folders. |
| 3. Number line | A tram line with only some stops labelled; maps with different scales; zooming to a stretch far from zero. |
| 4. Neighbours | Next-door houses, "the neighbour of the one whose neighbour is 17", and 1 000 next to 999 across a block. The witness lives at `n + 1`. |
| 5. Ranges | "Even side, past 230, before 240." `<` or `≤` decides whether one door is searched. Ordering suspects from clues. |
| 6. Rounding | A witness says "about 330", so 325 to 334. A newspaper rounds. Two rough clues pin down one house. |
| 7. Adding, subtracting | Ledgers: in, out, and what's missing. Pairs that make round totals show who worked with whom. |
| 8. Multiplying | Windows on a facade, lamps along a street; a count too large to do by hand. |
| 9. Distributivity | Two witnesses give the same count differently, `5 · (3 + 4)` and `5 · 3 + 5 · 4`, so they agree. A third says `5 · 3 + 4` and is lying. Judged without calculating, even for `409 · 86 − 309 · 86` and `100 · 86`. |
| 10. Division, remainder | 120 crates, 20 per cart, is 6 carts. A witness saw 5: where's the sixth? "Crates of 6, 7 left over" is a forgery, because that makes another crate. Both meanings of division get their own clues. |
| 11. Order of operations | Kristijan writes addresses as expressions. `100 − 20 · 3 + 10 : 2` is house 45; left to right gives 125, where the automatons are searching. Penkala's Book reads left to right too. Bracket locks on doors. |
| 12. Mean | The first part's scale returns as a seesaw. "On average 38 a day in September" means 1 140 in all. Fair share and balance both. |
| 13. Charts | Lamp oil by street, where one street burns at night; deliveries by day, where one day doubles. Reading beyond the data finds the hiding place. A chart whose axis step fools you. |

### Design challenges

- Clue checking needs new notebook forms, such as ranges and sets, plus a people-and-doors layer in the town.
- Saves must carry over: his live save sits at the current ending.
- Every clue must be clear to a 10-year-old, and detective logic can get wordy. Short lines only.
- The Track format has no research behind it, so its first street must show whether clues are clearer or muddier than rooms.

### Open questions

- Is the little automaton taken near the finale? Not needed until the later chapters are planned.
- Does the story finish at the end of 5th grade? Assumed yes, for Luka to veto.

- This part is one episode of six. It ends on a near miss and hands over to divisibility.
